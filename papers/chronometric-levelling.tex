\hypertarget{why-clocks-are-the-right-instrument}{%
\subsection{1. Why clocks are the right
instrument}\label{why-clocks-are-the-right-instrument}}

The framework\textquotesingle s primary observable is the proper-time
rate \textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash). Its central
relation is that acceleration is a bias along the gradient of that rate:

\textbackslash{[}a =
c\^{}\{2\}\textbackslash,\textbackslash frac\{d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)\}\{dr\}\textbackslash{]}

A clock at one geopotential and a clock at another tick at different
rates, and the ratio of their frequencies \emph{is} the difference in
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash). No model of the
intervening mass is required: the instrument reads the quantity
directly.

This matters for what follows. A measurement of
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) is not an inference
about mass, and carries no assumption about the distribution of matter
between the clocks. It tests the framework\textquotesingle s relation on
its own terms.

\hypertarget{three-published-results}{%
\subsection{2. Three published results}\label{three-published-results}}

The chronometric-levelling literature reports the fractional rate
difference in the standard form

\textbackslash{[}\textbackslash frac\{\textbackslash Delta\textbackslash nu\}\{\textbackslash nu\}
= \textbackslash frac\{1\}\{2\}\textbackslash,(1 +
\textbackslash alpha)\textbackslash,\textbackslash frac\{\textbackslash Delta
U\}\{c\^{}\{2\}\}\textbackslash{]}

where \textbackslash(\textbackslash Delta U\textbackslash) is the
geopotential difference and
\textbackslash(\textbackslash alpha\textbackslash) the dimensionless
parameter measuring departure from the Einstein value. The
framework\textquotesingle s \textbackslash(\textbackslash alpha =
0\textbackslash) limit is the Einstein value by construction: the
time-gradient law reproduces the observed rate difference with no free
parameter.

\hypertarget{beyond-clocks-gradients-and-differential-tests}{%
\subsection{4. Beyond clocks: gradients and differential
tests}\label{beyond-clocks-gradients-and-differential-tests}}

Clocks read one thing, the first spatial derivative of the time-density
rate. Two other kinds of local experiment read the rest, and the
framework predicts values for both without any cosmological input.

\textbf{The second derivative.} A gravity gradiometer measures
\textbackslash(T\_\{zz\} =
d\^{}\{2\}(d\textbackslash tau/dt)/dz\^{}\{2\}\textbackslash) directly.
Overstreet et al. built a dual-species atom interferometer that reaches
a relative precision of \textbackslash(\textbackslash Delta g/g
\textbackslash approx 6\textbackslash times10\^{}\{-11\}\textbackslash)
per shot and suppresses gravity-gradient systematics to one part in
\textbackslash(10\^{}\{13\}\textbackslash). That is the
framework\textquotesingle s observable at second order, read by atoms in
free fall, rather than by clocks at rest. The corresponding cold-atom
instruments reach comparable sensitivity in the field.

\textbf{The differential test.} Two bodies at the same place see the
same \textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash), so the
framework predicts identical acceleration and therefore
\textbackslash(\textbackslash eta = 0\textbackslash) exactly. MICROSCOPE
tested this and found
\textbackslash(\textbackslash eta(\textbackslash mathrm\{Ti\},\textbackslash mathrm\{Pt\})
= {[}-1.5 \textbackslash pm 2.3\_\{stat\} \textbackslash pm
1.5\_\{syst\}{]}\textbackslash times10\^{}\{-15\}\textbackslash), a null
at \textbackslash(2.7\textbackslash times10\^{}\{-15\}\textbackslash).
Because the prediction is a null by construction, the test has power
against any field variation across the 21 cm instrument separation, and
none against the field\textquotesingle s existence.

\textbf{An absolute gradient.} A cold-atom absolute gravimeter at the
Larzac observatory measured a vertical gravity gradient of
\textbackslash(-3.226 \textbackslash pm 0.017\textbackslash) kE over 1.2
m, which is \textbackslash(-2g/R\textbackslash) to 99 \%. This is a
measurement of the first derivative of
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) by matter rather
than by clocks, and it is the one place where the
framework\textquotesingle s quantity is compared against a terrestrial
value directly.

\textbf{The honest reading, which is not a confirmation.}

Every one of these is an upper limit, and none requires a non-zero
value. Taken together they push \textbackslash(c\_\{g\}\textbackslash)
toward zero, which means the framework is indistinguishable from the
limit it reduces to across every local test currently available. In all
three kinds of experiment the framework\textquotesingle s prediction
coincides with \textbackslash(a = g\textbackslash). What the data
establish is that the framework passes every test it faces without
contradiction, not that the field does anything.

This is the same limitation the withdrawn halo fit in the rotation-curve
paper had, and it should be stated in the same terms: a test that is
insensitive to the parameter cannot confirm the parameter. The
difference is that the clocks were not chosen to satisfy the framework,
so passing them is weak evidence rather than none, whereas the halo fit
passed by construction.

The singularity result does not depend on this. It rests on
\textbackslash(G\textbackslash) diverging at the horizon, so
\textbackslash(c\_\{g\}G = 1\textbackslash) is reached for any
\textbackslash(c\_\{g\} \textgreater{} 0\textbackslash); a small
\textbackslash(c\_\{g\}\textbackslash) means the divergence is steep,
not that the coupling is absent. The two statements are consistent, and
the framework has not yet done the work to separate them.

What would separate them is a test in which the field and the mass
disagree rather than a null. A measurement of the second derivative
resolving the difference between
\textbackslash(c\^{}\{2\}d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)/dr\textbackslash) and
\textbackslash(g\textbackslash) would do it. No such measurement exists
at present, and the current generation of gradiometers is not yet at the
required precision.

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\subsection{5. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}}

The relation \textbackslash(a =
c\^{}\{2\}\textbackslash,d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)/dr\textbackslash) is
what these experiments test, because
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) is what the clocks
measure. At 450 m the GR-violation parameter is consistent with the
framework\textquotesingle s zero-correction limit at 0.15σ. At 457 km
two independent methods agree on the same time-density difference to
0.85σ. At 1415 km the residual rate after the potential is removed is
consistent with zero.

No cosmological model, no mass function, and no fitted parameter enters
any of the three. The law reproduces the directly measured rate
difference at every baseline where the framework\textquotesingle s own
quantity has been read.

§4 widens this to the second derivative and to differential tests. Read
together, the local data bound nothing at all on the coupling, and the
reason is not limited sensitivity. A universal
\textbackslash(c\_\{g\}\textbackslash) enters every one of these
experiments as a common mode: it cancels in the MICROSCOPE mass ratio,
and it enters a clock comparison only as a factor-of-two correction to
the potential difference already measured. All four are consistent with
the framework\textquotesingle s zero-correction limit, and none of them
can distinguish it from the limit itself. The framework is not
contradicted by any local measurement, and is not distinguished by one
either. That is the state of the evidence, stated without inflation.

What is genuinely constrained is the singularity result, which follows
from the divergence of \textbackslash(G\textbackslash) at the horizon
and holds for every \textbackslash(c\_\{g\} \textgreater{}
0\textbackslash) regardless of how small the coupling is. Local
experiments bound the coupling; they do not touch the divergence.
