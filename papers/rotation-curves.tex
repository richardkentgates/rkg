\hypertarget{what-this-paper-claims-and-what-it-does-not}{%
\subsection{1. What this paper claims, and what it does
not}\label{what-this-paper-claims-and-what-it-does-not}}

The framework derives acceleration as a directional bias along the local
gradient of the proper-time rate:

\textbackslash{[}a =
c\^{}\{2\}\textbackslash,\textbackslash frac\{d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)\}\{dr\}\textbackslash{]}

The observable here is the time-density rate
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash), not a mass.
Distance necessitates time, so that quantity is defined everywhere and
measurable anywhere, and the law is a differential relation: it needs
two points, not a galaxy. That locality is what makes the framework
testable at all.

This paper makes one claim and states one requirement.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The claim.} SPARC kinematics require a pressureless component.
  Baryons fall short of the observed circular velocity in the outer
  halves of the overwhelming majority of these galaxies, and that is a
  statement about the data. It holds whatever the theory is.
\item
  \textbf{The requirement.} The framework, as currently specified,
  cannot answer it. This paper states what a field-based answer would
  have to reproduce, so the next step is an equation rather than another
  fit.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What was withdrawn from this paper, and why.}

An earlier version fitted a pressureless halo profile in quadrature with
the baryons, one free parameter per galaxy, and reported that the median
residual fell from 33.5 to 10.1~km/s. That fit has been removed rather
than reinterpreted, for three reasons that are worth recording.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{It could not distinguish the framework from what it reduces
  to.} The halo was computed as \textbackslash(V\^{}\{2\} =
  G\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{eff\}\}M(\textless r)/r\textbackslash),
  which is Newtonian gravity with a symbol attached. The paper itself
  noted that the coupling drops out, and \textbackslash(c\_\{g\}G
  \textbackslash sim 10\^{}\{-5\}\textbackslash) at galactic field
  amplitudes is below the MICROSCOPE bound. A test whose outcome is
  insensitive to the framework is not a test of the framework.
\item
  \textbf{The profile was chosen, not derived.} A mass parameter per
  galaxy supplies whatever shape is needed to reach the data. The data
  establish that the baryons are insufficient; they do not establish the
  form of whatever fills the gap, and inserting a halo asserts that form
  rather than predicting it.
\item
  \textbf{It was mass-defined reasoning in a framework whose primitive
  is not mass.} Inferring a halo mass from a velocity and fitting its
  profile is the classical operation. The framework treats mass as the
  terminal limit of time-density, so the question is what the
  field\textquotesingle s gradient does, not how much mass to add.
\end{itemize}

The 36 \% shortfall survives all of this. It was never a framework
result; it is kinematics, and it is reported below on its own terms.

\hypertarget{the-requirement-measured}{%
\subsection{2. The requirement,
measured}\label{the-requirement-measured}}

SPARC publishes the gas, disk and bulge contributions separately, so the
baryonic circular velocity is

\textbackslash{[}V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{bar\}\} =
\textbackslash sqrt\{V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{gas\}\}\^{}\{2\} +
V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{disk\}\}\^{}\{2\} +
V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{bul\}\}\^{}\{2\}\}\textbackslash{]}

The sample is 175 late-type galaxies with Spitzer photometry at 3.6~µm
and rotation curves from H~I and Hα spectroscopy, spanning more than
five decades in stellar mass. Of these, 165 carry enough radial coverage
for the outer-half comparison. Comparing the baryonic prediction with
the observed velocity across the outer half of each
galaxy\textquotesingle s radial range gives

\textbackslash{[}\textbackslash frac\{\textbackslash langle
V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{obs\}\} -
V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{bar\}\}\textbackslash rangle\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{outer\}\}\}\{\textbackslash langle
V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{obs\}\}\textbackslash rangle\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{outer\}\}\}
= +0.364\textbackslash{]}

Baryons account for roughly two-thirds of the observed circular velocity
at large radius, and they fall short in the overwhelming majority of
galaxies. A pressureless component is required. This conclusion is
independent of any theory: it is what the kinematics say.

Two things follow from it, and one does not. It follows that any account
of galactic rotation has to supply the missing attraction, whatever that
account is. It follows that the shortfall is uniform enough across the
sample to be a structural requirement rather than a few outliers. What
it does not follow is the form, the profile shape, or the mass of
whatever supplies it.

\hypertarget{why-the-framework-cannot-yet-answer-it}{%
\subsection{3. Why the framework cannot yet answer
it}\label{why-the-framework-cannot-yet-answer-it}}

The framework has a field, a potential
\textbackslash(V(\textbackslash phi)\textbackslash), and a coupling.
What it lacks is a spatial profile. The law \textbackslash(a =
c\^{}\{2\}d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)/dr\textbackslash) needs
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) as a function of
position, and the corpus specifies only the field\textquotesingle s
amplitude through the expansion history, not how it varies across a
disc.

This is a specification gap, not a computational one. It is worth being
precise about the difference, because the computational reading is
discouraging and the specification reading is not.

Resolving a galactic volume at the smallest measurable distance is
\textbackslash(7.9\textbackslash times10\^{}\{167\}\textbackslash)
cells, which is not computable: even at an idealised
\textbackslash(10\^{}\{12\}\textbackslash) cell-solves per second it is
\textbackslash(1.8\textbackslash times10\^{}\{147\}\textbackslash) Gyr,
some \textbackslash(10\^{}\{145\}\textbackslash) times the age of the
universe. A brute-force treatment is therefore impossible, and no amount
of arithmetic changes that.

But a missing function is not made whole by computing. If the
field\textquotesingle s profile on galactic scales is unspecified, then
evaluating the law on a finer grid resolves the same unspecified
function more finely. The obstruction is that the equation is not
written, not that the machine is too slow. That distinction is what
makes this paper a specification rather than a disappointment.

\hypertarget{what-a-field-based-account-would-have-to-reproduce}{%
\subsection{4. What a field-based account would have to
reproduce}\label{what-a-field-based-account-would-have-to-reproduce}}

For circular motion the acceleration must equal
\textbackslash(V\^{}\{2\}/r\textbackslash), so the
framework\textquotesingle s law requires

\textbackslash{[}\textbackslash frac\{d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)\}\{dr\}
=
\textbackslash frac\{V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{flat\}\}\^{}\{2\}\}\{c\^{}\{2\}\textbackslash,r\}\textbackslash{]}

A flat rotation curve at a constant \textbackslash(V\textbackslash) is
therefore a specific statement about the field:
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) must fall as
\textbackslash(1/r\textbackslash) across the outer disc, with no free
parameter. That is a target, and it is a testable one, because the
observed
\textbackslash(V\_\{\textbackslash mathrm\{flat\}\}\textbackslash)
varies by an order of magnitude across the sample and the corresponding
\textbackslash(1/r\textbackslash) field profile varies with it. A single
field profile that reproduced all 165 would be doing real work, and one
that did not would be informative.

The last row is the one that was violated before. A parameter fitted per
galaxy guarantees a fit; what would be informative is a field profile
with no per-galaxy freedom that either reproduces the curves or does
not.

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\subsection{5. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}}

Baryons fall 36 \% short of the observed circular velocity in the outer
half of 142 of 165 galaxies, with a median residual of 33.5~km/s.
Something beyond the baryons supplies that attraction. This is measured,
it is uniform across the sample, and it is independent of any theory.

The framework\textquotesingle s own law, \textbackslash(a =
c\^{}\{2\}d(d\textbackslash tau/dt)/dr\textbackslash), would supply it
if the field\textquotesingle s profile on galactic scales were
specified, since a flat curve requires
\textbackslash(d\textbackslash tau/dt\textbackslash) to fall as
\textbackslash(1/r\textbackslash). The corpus does not specify it. No
prediction is therefore made here, and the earlier halo fit is withdrawn
because a free mass parameter per galaxy could not have distinguished
the framework from the Newtonian limit it reduces to at galactic field
amplitudes.

The singularity result in the companion paper does not depend on any of
this. It follows from the field\textquotesingle s time-gradient
diverging as the Schwarzschild radius is approached, so the effective
coupling reaches zero before a horizon forms, and that holds for every
positive coupling regardless of the field\textquotesingle s spatial
profile. The galactic question is separate and remains open.
